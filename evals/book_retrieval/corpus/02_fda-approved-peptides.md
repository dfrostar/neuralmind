# Chapter 2: The FDA-Approved Peptides You Should Know

## Maria Gets a Prescription

Maria's doctor has written her a prescription for something she'd never heard of three weeks ago. The name sounds vaguely pharmaceutical — all consonants and hard syllables — but she's about to learn it's a peptide. The same technology her friend has been losing weight on. The same class of drug her neighbor takes for diabetes. The same molecular category that's probably sold your aunt something on Facebook.

She's confused. She's hopeful. She's wondering what she's actually putting into her body.

Let's make sure you know — before you ever pick up that prescription.

This chapter is about the peptides that made the big time. The ones with FDA approval. The ones with billions in sales and a trail of clinical trials behind them. In the grey-market world, almost nothing is certain. But in this chapter? Every claim is backed by evidence you can look up yourself.

## Semaglutide: The Drug That Changed Everything

Let's start with the heavyweight. The one you've definitely heard of. The drug that changed how the world thinks about weight loss.

Semaglutide — sold as Ozempic for diabetes and Wegovy for weight management — is a GLP-1 receptor agonist. <!-- claim: C-001 --> That's a dense phrase, so let's translate what it actually means inside your body.

You eat dinner. Your gut releases a natural hormone called GLP-1. That hormone does three things: it tells your stomach to slow down so you feel full longer, it signals your brain to reduce appetite, and it helps your pancreas release insulin when your blood sugar is high. <!-- claim: C-042 --> Then, within a couple of minutes, the hormone degrades and the signal disappears. That's how nature built you.

Semaglutide is a synthetic copy of that hormone — but with a crucial upgrade. Scientists modified it so it lasts in your body for a week instead of two minutes. One injection on Monday, and you have a steady "I just ate" signal running through your system all week. Not a hunger-crushing sledgehammer — more like a persistent, gentle reminder that you're full. Imagine having a waiter who politely tells you you've had enough before you reach for the dessert menu. Every meal. Every day.

The results have been staggering — and they're backed by some of the most rigorous clinical trials ever conducted in obesity medicine.

In the STEP 1 trial, nearly two thousand adults with overweight or obesity received semaglutide 2.4 mg once weekly. At 68 weeks, the average weight loss was 14.9% from baseline. <!-- claim: C-002 --> That's not a typo. Nearly fifteen percent of your body weight, on average, from a weekly injection. For someone weighing 200 pounds, that's about 30 pounds lost. Some people lose much more; some lose less. But the average is real, and it's unprecedented for a drug.

Then came the SELECT trial — a study that changed how we think about these drugs entirely. Over seventeen thousand adults with overweight or obesity and established cardiovascular disease were followed for years. None of them had diabetes. The question: would semaglutide reduce heart attacks and strokes in this population? The answer: yes. A 20% reduction in major adverse cardiovascular events — heart attacks, strokes, cardiovascular death — compared to placebo. <!-- claim: C-003 -->

Think about what that means. A weight loss drug that also protects your heart. Not because it lowers cholesterol or blood pressure directly — though it may help with both — but because the underlying biology of obesity and inflammation is being addressed at the source. This isn't just a drug for your bathroom scale. It's a drug for your life expectancy.

But here's the part nobody's putting on a billboard.

If you stop semaglutide, the weight comes back. How much? About two-thirds of what you lost, within one year. <!-- claim: C-004 --> The STEP 1 extension study tracked participants after they stopped the drug, and the results were sobering. The drug doesn't cure obesity — it manages it. Stop the management, and the biology returns. This is not a failure of the drug. It's a feature of how your body works. Your body wants to return to its set point. The drug holds the door; you still have to walk through it.

And the side effects are real. Gastrointestinal issues dominate: nausea affects about 44% of patients, diarrhea about 30%, vomiting about 24%. <!-- claim: C-009 --> Between 60 and 80% of people experience some GI effects, especially when starting or increasing the dose. Most people manage them by starting at a low dose, eating smaller meals, avoiding fatty or fried foods, and letting their body adapt over weeks. For a small minority, the side effects are too much — and that's okay. There are other options.

There's also a black box warning — the FDA's most serious label — for thyroid C-cell tumors. This is based on animal studies where rodents developed tumors at high doses. The human risk has not been established, and no clear signal has emerged in human trials or post-market surveillance. But it's there, and your doctor should know about it before prescribing. <!-- claim: C-010 -->

Here's the bottom line on semaglutide: it's the real deal. It's not a fad, it's not a scam, it's not snake oil in a syringe. It's the most effective weight loss drug ever approved, with cardiovascular benefits that go beyond the scale. But it's a long-term commitment, not a quick fix. You don't take it for three months and walk away transformed. You take it, you build new habits, you change your relationship with food — and then you keep taking it to maintain those changes.

And here's something we need to talk about directly. When you hear about semaglutide side effects — the nausea, the vomiting, the "Ozempic face" memes that dominated social media — remember the context. Every drug has side effects. The question is whether the benefit justifies the burden. For most people with obesity or type 2 diabetes, the answer with semaglutide has been yes. The trials are clear: the benefits — sustained weight loss, cardiovascular risk reduction, improved glycemic control — exceed the risks when prescribed and monitored appropriately.

## Tirzepatide: The Two-for-One Upgrade

Semaglutide changed the game. But scientists at Eli Lilly asked a question that would change it again: what if we could work two pathways instead of one?

The result is tirzepatide, sold as Mounjaro for diabetes and Zepbound for weight management. It's a dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist — which is science-speak for a drug that hits two different gut hormone systems at once. <!-- claim: C-005 -->

Here's the difference. Semaglutide mimics GLP-1 — one pathway, one mechanism. Tirzepatide does that and also mimics GIP — a second gut hormone that improves how your body processes fat and sugar. <!-- claim: C-043 --> Think of semaglutide as a one-lane highway: traffic flows, but there's a bottleneck. Tirzepatide adds a second lane. More traffic can move. More biology gets addressed.

In head-to-head trials published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2025, tirzepatide demonstrated superior weight loss compared to semaglutide — meaning people lost more weight on tirzepatide at equivalent doses. <!-- claim: C-006 --> This wasn't a marginal difference. It was statistically significant and clinically meaningful. For patients who didn't lose enough on semaglutide, tirzepatide became the logical next step.

The side effect profile is similar to semaglutide because the GLP-1 component — the one responsible for most of the GI effects — is still there. Nausea, diarrhea, vomiting are all possibilities, and the same slow-dose-escalation strategy applies. But the dual mechanism means you're getting more biological benefit for the same side effect burden. That's a trade worth examining with your doctor.

Tirzepatide is the drug that made Eli Lilly a trillion-dollar company. And for good reason. If semaglutide is a reliable sedan that gets you where you need to go, tirzepatide is the sports car upgrade. Same road, more horsepower.

## Retatrutide: The Triple Threat

![Figure 2.1 — How the Three Drugs Work. Comparison infographic: Semaglutide (single arrow, GLP-1 receptor), Tirzepatide (two arrows, GLP-1 + GIP), Retatrutide (three arrows, GLP-1 + GIP + Glucagon). Weight loss results: 14.9% → 20.3% → 24.2%.](assets/chapter-02/02a-pathway-comparison.jpg)

You've seen one pathway. You've seen two pathways. Now meet the drug that works three.

Retatrutide — not yet available at your pharmacy, but coming soon — is a triple agonist. It activates GLP-1 (appetite, blood sugar), GIP (fat and sugar processing), and glucagon (energy burning). <!-- claim: C-044 -->

That third pathway, glucagon, is what makes retatrutide different. Glucagon tells your body to burn stored fat for energy and increases how many calories you burn at rest. Not through exercise. Not through willpower. Through a molecular signal that says, "It's time to tap the reserves." <!-- claim: C-044 -->

The Phase 2 results, published in 2023, were eye-opening. Participants achieved up to 24.2% weight loss at 48 weeks. <!-- claim: C-007 --> Let me put that in perspective. Bariatric surgery — a permanent, irreversible, major surgical procedure — produces about 25 to 30% weight loss on average. Retatrutide is approaching surgical-level results with a weekly injection. That's not a small deal. That is, potentially, a paradigm shift.

But — and this is a big but — retatrutide is NOT yet FDA-approved. Phase 3 trials are ongoing as of mid-2026. <!-- claim: C-008 --> The earliest it could hit the market is probably 2027 or 2028, assuming the Phase 3 data holds up. And Phase 3 is where drugs go to fail. Many promising Phase 2 results don't survive the larger, longer, more rigorous Phase 3 trials.

There's also the question of side effects. Three pathways mean three sources of potential trouble. The GI effects from GLP-1. The fat processing changes from GIP. And glucagon's energy-burning effect can cause increased heart rate and a feeling of restlessness. <!-- claim: C-045 --> More power, more side effects to manage. The trade-off is real, and clinical teams are still learning how to optimize dosing to minimize both.

Retatrutide represents the future of obesity pharmacotherapy. But it's not the present. If you're reading this and thinking about starting therapy today, retatrutide isn't an option yet. Semaglutide and tirzepatide are. And they work very well.

## Liraglutide: The Original That Showed Us the Way

Before semaglutide made headlines, there was liraglutide.

Sold as Victoza for diabetes and Saxenda for weight management, liraglutide is a GLP-1 agonist — the same mechanism as semaglutide, but an older version. <!-- claim: C-046 --> It works the same way: slows stomach emptying, reduces appetite, helps the pancreas release insulin. But there are two key differences.

First, liraglutide requires a daily injection instead of weekly. That's a meaningful difference in convenience and adherence. Imagine remembering to take a medication every day versus every week — the weekly option is just easier to build into a routine.

Second, liraglutide doesn't achieve the same level of weight loss as semaglutide or tirzepatide. The newer drugs are simply more effective. This isn't a knock on liraglutide — it was the proof of concept that GLP-1 agonists could work for weight management. It paved the way. But if you're starting fresh today, your doctor would likely recommend semaglutide or tirzepatide over liraglutide.

Liraglutide is still prescribed. It still works. It's still FDA-approved. But it's been superseded by better options. Think of it as the iPhone 4. It was revolutionary in its day. But you wouldn't buy one today when the iPhone 15 exists.

If your doctor prescribes liraglutide, there's probably a reason — insurance formulary, prior authorization hurdles, or a specific clinical consideration. Ask why. The answer might be a good one. Or it might be time to ask about a newer option.

## The Other Peptides on the FDA Roster

The GLP-1 drugs get all the headlines, but they're not the only peptides with FDA approval. Two others deserve your attention because they illustrate just how wide the peptide platform can stretch — from the bottom of the ocean to the inside of your bones.

## Ziconotide: The Cone Snail That Kills Pain

Now for something completely different.

Not every FDA-approved peptide is about metabolism and weight. Some of them come from the most unexpected places — like the ocean floor.

Ziconotide — brand name Prialt — is a synthetic version of cone snail venom. <!-- claim: C-047 --> Yes, you read that right. Cone snails are those beautiful, deadly sea creatures with harpoon-like teeth that inject venom to paralyze their prey. Scientists isolated the active peptide, synthesized it in a lab, and turned it into a drug for chronic pain.

Here's how it works. Your body feels pain when signals travel from the injury site up your spinal cord to your brain. Ziconotide blocks calcium channels in the spinal cord — essentially cutting that signal before it reaches your brain. <!-- claim: C-047 --> The pain still exists at the injury site, but your brain never hears about it.

This is huge for one specific reason: it's non-opioid. There's no addiction risk, no respiratory depression, no tolerance that forces higher and higher doses. For patients with severe chronic pain who haven't responded to anything else — and especially for patients who've struggled with opioid dependence — ziconotide offers a completely different approach.

The catch? It requires a surgically implanted pump that delivers the drug directly to the spinal fluid. <!-- claim: C-047 --> This is not a pill you swallow or an injection you give yourself in the belly. It's a serious intervention for a serious condition. Ziconotide is reserved for the most difficult pain cases — the ones where everything else has failed.

But it's a perfect example of what makes peptides special. They can do things other drugs can't. They can be precise. They can target specific pathways. In this case, a peptide from a sea creature can kill pain without the risk of addiction. That's not incremental innovation. That's a fundamentally different approach.

## Teriparatide: The Bone Builder

One more stop before we step back and look at the big picture.

Teriparatide — brand name Forteo — is the only osteoporosis drug that actually builds new bone. <!-- claim: C-048 --> Every other osteoporosis drug works by slowing down bone loss. They're like putting a bandage on a leaking pipe. Teriparatide is different. It rebuilds the pipe.

It works by activating osteoblasts — the cells that build bone — through the parathyroid hormone receptor. <!-- claim: C-048 --> Think of osteoblasts as construction workers. Most osteoporosis drugs tell the construction workers to stop tearing things down. Teriparatide tells them to start building.

This is a daily injection, typically prescribed for up to two years. After that, patients usually switch to a maintenance drug that preserves the bone they've rebuilt. The results are significant increases in bone density and meaningful reductions in fracture risk — which for an elderly patient with severe osteoporosis can mean the difference between independence and a hip replacement.

Teriparatide is proof that peptides can do more than signal and regulate. They can rebuild. They can restore. They can reverse damage that every other class of drug can only slow down.

## What These Drugs Have in Common

Look at this list and you'll see a pattern. Peptides aren't one thing. They're a platform. Each one is a different message sent to a different part of your body using the same basic technology.

Semaglutide says: "You're full. Stop eating."
Tirzepatide says: "You're full. And process that fat more efficiently."
Retatrutide says: "You're full. Process fat better. And burn some stored energy while you're at it."
Ziconotide says: "You're in pain, but we're not going to let your brain hear about it."
Teriparatide says: "Build bone."

Same platform. Different messages. Different results.

And here's the thing: these aren't the end of the story. The pipeline of FDA-approved peptides extends far beyond what we've covered here. Pluvicto — a radiopharmaceutical peptide — delivers radioactive payloads directly to prostate cancer cells, killing them while sparing healthy tissue. <!-- claim: C-061 --> Danuglipron is an oral GLP-1 agonist from Pfizer that doesn't require injection at all, still in development but pointing toward a future where peptide therapy could be as simple as swallowing a pill. <!-- claim: C-060 --> New delivery methods — nasal sprays, patches, implants — are all in active development. <!-- claim: C-078 -->

But for now, these are the stars. The ones with FDA approval. The ones with evidence behind them. The ones your doctor can actually prescribe.

## What "FDA-Approved" Actually Means

Before we move on, let's be honest about what that label does and doesn't mean.

"FDA-approved" means the drug manufacturer submitted evidence from rigorous clinical trials showing that the benefits outweigh the risks for a specific condition. It means the FDA reviewed that evidence and agreed. <!-- claim: C-083 -->

It does NOT mean:
- The drug is safe for everyone (every drug has side effects)
- The drug works for every condition (approval is condition-specific)
- The drug has been studied for decades (some have; some have only been studied for years)
- You won't regain weight if you stop taking it (you almost certainly will)

Approval is not a guarantee. It's a signal — one of the strongest signals in medicine — that a treatment is backed by real evidence. But it's not a promise. Always read what the evidence actually says, not just the headline.

Peptides are not steroids. They do not force your body to do anything — they signal, they suggest, they amplify what your body already knows how to do. <!-- claim: C-080 --> They treat diabetes, obesity, osteoporosis, chronic pain, and rare genetic diseases. They are not just for bodybuilders. They are for anyone whose body needs a better message. <!-- claim: C-082 -->
