## Disclaimer — Please Read This First

Let's get the serious part out of the way before we get to the fun part.

This book is an educational resource. It is not medical advice. It is not a prescription. It is not a treatment protocol. It is not a substitute for the judgment of a licensed healthcare provider who has examined you, taken your history, reviewed your labs, and considered your specific circumstances. Nothing in these pages is intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.

Here is what this means in practice. You may read about a peptide that sounds like it was designed specifically for your condition. You may feel the urge to order it online, reconstitute it with bacteriostatic water, and inject it based on a protocol you found on the internet. Please do not do that. The peptide therapy landscape is cluttered with grey-market suppliers, unregulated compounds, sketchy dosing recommendations, and well-meaning community forums where anecdote is mistaken for evidence. A peptide that changes one patient's life can send another to the emergency room — because we are all biochemically different, because supply-chain purity varies wildly, and because the difference between a therapeutic dose and a dangerous one can be measured in micrograms.

The law reinforces this distinction. In the United States, most peptides are not FDA-approved for the indications patients commonly use them for. The FDA has approved a small handful of peptide drugs — things like semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy), tirzepatide (Mounjaro, Zepbound), and a few others — for specific, labeled uses. Everything else exists in a grey zone: compounded at licensed pharmacies under less stringent oversight, sold as research chemicals not intended for human consumption, or imported from overseas manufacturers whose quality control you cannot verify. The chapters that follow will walk you through exactly where each therapy lives on that spectrum, but the baseline assumption should be: *if it wasn't approved for your condition by a regulatory agency, you are taking on additional, uncounted risk.*

The authors have made every effort to ensure the accuracy of the information presented here as of the publication date. Science moves fast — peptide research especially so. What was accepted clinical practice at the time of writing may be outdated by the time you read this. We have cited our sources throughout, and we encourage you to verify claims against the most current peer-reviewed literature. A good place to start is PubMed (pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov), which is free and searchable by anyone.

Neither Darren Frost nor Rye Estepp, MD, nor any affiliated institution, shall be held liable for any loss, injury, claim, or damage arising from the use or misuse of the information contained in this book. Your health decisions are yours. Make them with your doctor, not with Google, not with a reddit thread, and not — respectfully — with this book alone. Consider this a conversation starter to have with a qualified professional, not a conclusion in itself.

## Evidence Tiering Legend

Throughout this book, you will see bracketed labels next to claims: [RCT], [Preclinical], [Clinical], or [PWC]. These are not academic decorations. They are our promise that we will never pretend a weak claim is a strong one. Here is what each label means.

**[RCT] — Randomized Controlled Trial**

This claim is supported by at least one randomized controlled trial in human subjects. An RCT is the gold standard in medical evidence: real people, randomly assigned to treatment or control, with outcomes measured objectively. When you see [RCT], you are looking at evidence that meets the same standard the FDA uses to approve a drug. It is not perfect — no single study is — but it is the best kind of evidence we have. Examples include the landmark trials for semaglutide and tirzepatide, which enrolled thousands of patients across multiple years.

**[Preclinical] — Animal or Laboratory Studies Only**

This claim is supported by animal studies, cell-culture experiments, or in-vitro data — but not yet by human clinical trials. Preclinical evidence is where promising therapies are born, but it is also where the gap between "works in a petri dish" and "works in a person" is widest. Many peptides with compelling preclinical profiles never make it through human trials. When you see [Preclinical], treat it as interesting, hypothesis-generating, and worth watching — not as a basis for treatment decisions.

**[Clinical] — Clinical Experience and Expert Consensus**

This claim is supported by the accumulated clinical experience of practitioners who use peptide therapy regularly, by open-label studies, case series, or retrospective analyses. Clinical evidence is messier than RCT data — it lacks the controlled conditions and randomization that protect against bias — but it reflects what happens when treatments meet real patients in real clinics. When the same clinical observation appears across dozens of independent practitioners, it carries weight even without a formal trial. But it also carries more uncertainty than [RCT].

**[PWC] — Peptide World Congress Lecture Content**

This claim originates from presentations, panels, or discussions at the Peptide World Congress, the premier professional conference for peptide researchers and clinicians. PWC content represents the cutting edge of the field — emerging data, ongoing trials, clinical anecdotes shared among experts. It is the least filtered category of evidence in this book, and we label it as such. When you see [PWC], you are reading something that is being actively debated among specialists, not something that has been settled by science. It is included because it is interesting and because the cutting edge is where the next breakthroughs live — but it should be read with the most caution of any tier.

*One more thing about these labels.* Evidence tiers describe the *quantity and quality* of supporting data, not the size of the effect or the likelihood that a therapy works for you personally. A peptide backed by multiple RCTs may still not be right for your specific situation. A peptide with only preclinical data might, someday, turn out to be transformative — but betting on that today is a gamble, not a medical decision. We will tell you what the evidence says, and we will tell you where it stops. What you do from there is between you and your doctor.

## How to Read This Book

This is not a textbook, and you do not need to read it cover to cover. It is designed to be useful in three different ways.

If you are brand new to peptide therapy, start with Chapter One. That chapter explains what peptides are, how they work at the cellular level, and why they have become one of the fastest-growing categories in modern medicine. It will give you the vocabulary and the conceptual framework you need for everything that follows. Think of it as Peptide Biology 101, compressed into language that assumes you have never taken a chemistry class.

If you already know the basics and are trying to evaluate a specific therapy — maybe your doctor recommended BPC-157 for gut healing, or you are curious about the difference between semaglutide and tirzepatide — skip ahead to the individual chapters. Each peptide or peptide class is discussed in its own section with its own evidence tiering. The claim markers are designed to let you scan quickly: look for the [RCT] tags if you want the strongest evidence, or check the [Preclinical] and [PWC] sections if you are trying to understand where the hype ends and the data begins.

If you are a clinician or a particularly diligent patient, the recommended reading section at the back of the book provides full citations for the major studies we reference. You can follow the trail from our summary to the original paper in about two clicks. We encourage this. The best thing you can do after reading this book is verify what we have told you against the primary literature.

Throughout the book, you will notice a consistent voice. We use "we" to mean the authors and clinical teams behind this work — the collective effort of writing, researching, and practicing the medicine described here. We use "I" when Darren is speaking from personal experience or opinion. And we use "you" a lot, because this book is for you: the patient, the caregiver, the person trying to make a real decision about a real treatment.

You will also notice that we occasionally repeat ourselves. This is intentional. Some concepts — like the difference between a peptide approved for one indication and prescribed off-label for another — are important enough to deserve multiple explanations from different angles. Think of it as reinforcement, not redundancy.

Finally, a word on what this book is *not*. It is not a how-to guide for self-administration. It does not contain dosing protocols, reconstitution instructions, or compounding recipes. You will find no "peptide stacks" or "optimal cycles." These omissions are deliberate: that information, when it is accurate at all, belongs in a clinical setting, under the supervision of a licensed professional who can adjust for your body weight, kidney function, medication interactions, and dozens of other variables that no book can account for. This book will help you ask better questions. Your doctor will help you find the right answers.
