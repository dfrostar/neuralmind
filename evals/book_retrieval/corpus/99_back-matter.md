## Glossary of Key Terms

We've thrown a lot of vocabulary at you across these chapters. This glossary is your cheat sheet — quick definitions written the way we'd explain them over coffee, not like a medical dictionary.

**Agonist** — A molecule that binds to a receptor and activates it, like a key that not only fits the lock but turns it. Semaglutide is a GLP-1 receptor agonist: it fits into the GLP-1 receptor and tells your body to start the digestive response.

**Amino acid** — The building blocks of peptides and proteins. String twenty together and you've got a peptide; string a thousand and you've got a protein. Your body uses twenty standard amino acids to build nearly everything it needs.

**Bioavailability** — The percentage of a drug that reaches your bloodstream intact. Oral peptides struggle here — your stomach and liver degrade most of them before they can arrive where they're needed. This is why most peptide therapies are injected: subcutaneous delivery bypasses the digestive tract and delivers nearly 100% bioavailability. <!-- claim: C-096 -->

**Clinical experience** — Knowledge gained from doctors treating real patients in real clinics. Weaker than a controlled trial because there's no blinded comparison group, but stronger than lab data because these are actual human bodies responding to actual treatment.

**Compounding** — Preparing a customized medication for an individual patient. When a commercially available drug doesn't fit — you're allergic to a filler, need a different dose, or the drug is in shortage — a compounding pharmacy can make what you need.

**GHRH (Growth Hormone-Releasing Hormone)** — The natural hormone your brain sends to your pituitary to say, "Release growth hormone now." Synthetic versions like sermorelin and CJC-1295 mimic this signal.

**GLP-1 (Glucagon-Like Peptide-1)** — A gut hormone that slows stomach emptying, reduces appetite, and stimulates insulin release when blood sugar rises. This is the pathway behind semaglutide, tirzepatide, and the revolution in obesity treatment.

**Half-life** — The time it takes for half of a drug to be cleared from your body. A drug with a two-minute half-life needs an IV drip; semaglutide's engineered half-life of about a week means one injection covers you for seven days.

**Mitochondria** — The energy-producing structures inside your cells. Several peptides — MOTS-c, SS-31, humanin — work directly on mitochondria, improving efficiency and protecting them from damage.

**Peptide** — A chain of fewer than fifty amino acids. Smaller than a protein, bigger than a single amino acid. Peptides are nature's signaling molecules: they carry messages between cells, trigger healing, and regulate nearly every biological process.

**Peptide therapy** — The medical use of peptide molecules to treat, prevent, or manage health conditions. Unlike conventional drugs that are often synthetic compounds your body doesn't recognize, peptide therapy works with the body's existing signaling systems — using molecules that are either identical or very similar to ones your body already produces. This is why side effects are often mild and why the approach feels different from taking a pill for a symptom.

**Pharmacokinetics** — How a drug moves through your body: absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion. In plain terms: how fast it gets in, where it goes, how your body breaks it down, and how long until it leaves. Understanding pharmacokinetics is what separates a well-designed peptide protocol from a guessing game.

**Preclinical** — Research conducted in cells or animals, not humans. Promising and necessary before human trials begin, but a long way from proven. Preclinical gets you excited; RCT gets you convinced.

**RCT (Randomized Controlled Trial)** — The gold standard of medical evidence. Patients are randomly assigned to receive the drug or a placebo, and neither they nor their doctors know who got what. When we tell you semaglutide produced 14.9% average weight loss, that number came from an RCT. <!-- claim: C-002 -->

**Receptor** — A protein on the surface of a cell that receives signals from outside. Peptides bind to receptors the way a key fits a lock — but it's not a dead bolt. When the peptide turns the key, the receptor changes what that cell does.

**Reconstitution** — The process of turning powdered peptide into injectable liquid. Peptides are shipped as a freeze-dried powder because they're fragile — dissolved in water they'd degrade within days. You add bacteriostatic water, swirl gently (don't shake), and suddenly you have a solution ready for use. Your clinic will show you how, and it becomes second nature after the first time.

**Subcutaneous** — Just under the skin, into the layer of fat. This is where most peptide injections go. Subcutaneous tissue has a rich blood supply that absorbs the peptide gradually, giving steady levels over time. It's not as fast as IV, not as slow as a pill — it's the Goldilocks zone for peptide delivery.

**Telomere** — The protective cap at the end of each chromosome, like the plastic tip on a shoelace. Telomeres shorten as cells divide, and when they get too short, the cell ages. Epitalon activates telomerase, the enzyme that rebuilds them.

## How to Talk to Your Family About Peptide Therapy

You've read this book. You understand the evidence tiers, the biology, and the difference between an RCT and preclinical data. But when you bring up peptide therapy at the dinner table, you might get a look that says, "Are you sure that's safe?"

That's fair. Peptides sound intimidating, and the wellness industry has given plenty of reasons for healthy skepticism. Here's how to have the conversation without sounding like you've joined a fad.

**Start with what peptides actually are.** "Peptides are just short chains of amino acids — the same building blocks your body uses to make proteins. We already have them naturally. Peptide therapy just adds back what your body might not be making enough of." This reframes it from "mysterious chemical" to "biology your body already knows."

**Compare it to something familiar.** "It's similar to how someone with low thyroid takes thyroid hormone, or how a diabetic takes insulin. You're not adding something foreign — you're supplementing something your body's signal system is running short on."

**Acknowledge the uncertainty honestly.** If someone asks whether something is proven, don't overclaim. Say, "Some of it is well-studied — GLP-1s for weight loss have gold-standard trials. Other peptides have less evidence, and I'm weighing that before I decide." Your family doesn't need a PhD in evidence tiers, but they can hear the difference between someone who's done their homework and someone who's repeating marketing.

**Invite their concern instead of dismissing it.** "I'm glad you asked about safety — here's what I've learned about how these are monitored, what side effects look like, and how doctors handle them." When people feel heard, they're far more open to what you have to say.

And if you want to share this book with them? Go ahead. That's what it's for.

## What to Expect at Your First Peptide Therapy Appointment

Walking into a clinic for your first peptide consultation can feel different from a standard doctor's visit, and that's partly the point. You're not there for a five-minute prescription refill. You're there for an extended conversation about your health, your goals, and whether peptides are a reasonable tool to consider.

**Before the appointment**, you'll likely fill out a detailed health history questionnaire. Be thorough — past surgeries, current medications, allergies, supplements, sleep patterns, stress levels. Peptide therapy works best when the clinician has the full picture, not just the headline.

**During the appointment**, the clinician will walk through your history and talk with you about what you're hoping to address. This could be weight management, recovery from an injury, sleep quality, energy, cognitive function, or aging-related concerns. Expect them to ask "why" several times — they need to understand not just what you want, but what's driving it.

**The education part** is where this appointment differs most from a conventional one. A good peptide clinician will explain how the proposed peptide works, what evidence supports it, how you'll take it (likely subcutaneous injection, using a tiny needle you'll barely feel), what side effects to watch for, and how long before you might notice a difference. You should leave the appointment understanding the treatment well enough to explain it to someone else.

**The plan itself** might include labs first — blood work to establish baseline values before you start. Many clinics won't prescribe until they see your numbers. Others may start you on a low dose and ramp up. Your first dose might even be given in the clinic so you can be observed before taking it home.

**Take a notebook.** Seriously. There's a lot of information, and you'll remember maybe half of it without writing it down.

**Ask these questions:**
- What evidence level supports this peptide for my specific goal?
- What does the injection schedule look like?
- Are there any interactions with my current medications?
- How will we know if it's working, and when will we check?
- What's the plan if I have side effects?

A good clinic expects these questions. A great one welcomes them.

## Evidence Tier Quick Reference Card

Throughout this book, you've seen claims marked with bracketed labels: [RCT], [Preclinical], [Clinical], [PWC]. These aren't decorations — they're a quick way to gauge how much trust to put in each piece of information, without needing a PhD in research methodology. Here's a card you can tear out or screenshot:

| Label | Evidence Level | What It Means | Use It To |
|---|---|---|---|
| **[RCT]** | Gold Standard | Randomized controlled trial — humans, blinded, placebo-controlled, peer-reviewed | Make confident decisions |
| **[Clinical]** | Real-World Evidence | Observed in actual patients by clinicians; no control group | Guide discussion with your doctor |
| **[Preclinical]** | Lab Research | Cells or animal studies; not yet tested in humans | Understand what's being explored |
| **[PWC]** | Expert Frontier | Presented at Peptide World Congress; not peer-reviewed, but cutting-edge | Stay aware of emerging thinking |

**Our rule throughout:** only [RCT] claims are asserted as fact. [Preclinical] claims get hedged with "may" and "promising." [Clinical] and [PWC] claims are always attributed to their source. We've done the tracking; you get to read and know, at a glance, how strong the evidence is.

## Recommended Reading

This book is a starting point, not a destination. If you want to go deeper, here are the sources we trust — each paired with what you'll get from it.

***The Obesity Code*** by Dr. Jason Fung — The best explanation we've found for how insulin resistance drives weight gain, and why the standard "eat less, move more" advice misses the biology entirely. Fung writes for patients, not academics, and his framework around insulin, fasting, and metabolic health complements what you've learned about GLP-1s in this book. Read this if you want to understand the *why* behind metabolic disease, not just the *what* of treatment.

***Lifespan: Why We Age — and Why We Don't Have To*** by Dr. David Sinclair — The book that brought longevity science to the mainstream and made everyday readers care about sirtuins, NAD+, and cellular repair pathways. Read it for the big picture on how aging works at the molecular level; our book fills in the peptide-specific details. Sinclair's framework helps you see why peptides like epitalon (telomere maintenance) and MOTS-c (mitochondrial function) aren't random compounds — they're targeted tools for specific aging mechanisms.

***The Emperor of All Maladies*** by Siddhartha Mukherjee — A Pulitzer-winning biography of cancer that doubles as a masterclass in how medical science actually progresses. It will help you understand why some treatments take decades, why evidence standards matter, and why a single promising study isn't the same as proof. Every patient navigating a complex health decision should read this book — not because you have cancer, but because it teaches you how to think about medical claims.

***Outlive: The Science and Art of Longevity*** by Dr. Peter Attia — A more recent addition to the longevity canon that breaks aging down into four chronic disease "horsemen" and gives you a strategy for each. Attia's evidence-first approach and willingness to say "we don't know yet" aligns closely with the philosophy of this book. If you want a practical framework for thinking about your health span across decades, this is your companion volume.

For online resources, start with **PubMed** (pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) — the free database of peer-reviewed research. If it's not on PubMed, treat the claim with skepticism. **ClinicalTrials.gov** shows you which peptides are being studied and what the results were. **FDA.gov** covers approvals, safety alerts, and regulatory status. **MedlinePlus** (medlineplus.gov) offers the same source material written for people who aren't doctors. And **Google Scholar** (scholar.google.com) can help you find papers when you remember the author's name but not the journal.
