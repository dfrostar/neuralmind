# Conclusion: Where We Leave Maria

Maria started this book with a prescription she didn't understand, a head full of jargon, and a laptop that only made things worse. That was nine chapters ago.

She's not that person anymore.

If you've read this far, you've followed her journey from confusion to confidence — not because she became a biochemist, but because she learned to ask better questions. What is this drug? How does it work? What does the evidence say? What are the alternatives? What's the plan if this doesn't work? Those questions — not the answers, but the willingness to ask — transformed her relationship with her own health.

But Maria's story is really your story. That's the point.

---

Let's be honest about what this book has — and hasn't — done.

We haven't made peptides simple. We've made them understandable. There's a difference. "Simple" means leaving out the hard parts. "Understandable" means walking you through those hard parts without losing you along the way. You now know the difference between FDA approval and compounding, between a Phase 2 trial and a preclinical study, between a peptide that signals and a steroid that forces. You know that semaglutide's STEP 1 trial showed 14.9% weight loss at 68 weeks, that tirzepatide targets two pathways instead of one, and that BPC-157 works beautifully in rats but hasn't been tested in a single human RCT. You know what 503A and 503B mean, why the PCAC's July 2026 vote matters, and why "research chemical" is a legal category, not a safety endorsement.

That's a lot. And it's only the beginning.

Here's what we hope you'll carry forward.

**First, evidence is not static.** The peptides we described as "in development" when we wrote this chapter may be on pharmacy shelves by the time you read it. The regulatory landscape shifts. New trials publish. Old assumptions get overturned. The framework in this book — how to evaluate claims, how to talk to your doctor, how to distinguish hope from hype — is more durable than any single fact within it. Use the framework.

**Second, being informed is not the same as being skeptical to the point of cynicism.** There is real science here. Real patients are getting better. The peptide revolution is not marketing — it's clinical reality. But "real science" doesn't mean "unlimited claims," and "it works for some" doesn't mean "it works for everyone." Hold both truths at once: genuine excitement about what's possible, genuine caution about what remains unknown.

**Third, your voice matters in the exam room.** The research is clear: patients who ask questions, who bring evidence, who share their values and concerns — they get better care. Not because doctors are withholding it, but because medicine works best as a partnership. You don't need to be difficult. You need to be engaged. There's a difference.

Maria walked into her follow-up appointment three months after starting treatment and told her doctor something she'd never said before: "Here's what I'm experiencing, here's what I'm concerned about, and here's what I'd like to discuss." Her doctor leaned forward. Not back. They built a plan together.

That's the transformation. Not a prescription. Not a diagnosis. A partnership.

You've done the work. You've read the science, evaluated the evidence, and earned the right to have that conversation.

Now go have it.
