# Chapter 8: Questions to Ask Your Prescriber

## Maria Goes to Her Appointment

Maria sat in the exam room with her prescription in hand. She'd read this book cover to cover. She knew what GLP-1 receptors did. She understood the difference between FDA-approved and grey-market. She'd read about compounding regulations and the difference between 503A and 503B.

But sitting there, watching the clock tick, she felt her mind go blank.

You know that feeling. You walk into the exam room with a list of questions and walk out realizing you asked none of them. The doctor is busy, the room is cold, and you don't want to be the difficult patient.

Here's the truth: asking good questions doesn't make you difficult. It makes you informed. Informed patients get better care. <!-- claim: C-040 -->

Research on patient-centered communication — the science of how doctors and patients talk to each other — identifies four core skills that lead to better outcomes: eliciting the patient's perspective, understanding their context, reaching a shared understanding, and negotiating a plan together. <!-- claim: C-040 --> These aren't just nice-to-haves. They're the difference between a prescription you fill and a plan you actually follow.

So let's give you a framework. Think of this as your conversation guide — not a cross-examination, but a way to turn a rushed appointment into a partnership.

## Before Starting: The Questions That Lead Somewhere

![Figure 8.1 — Questions to Ask Your Doctor. Checklist card with 6 questions: FDA approval, evidence, side effects, alternatives, sourcing, cost.](assets/chapter-08/08a-checklist-card.jpg)

I'm going to give you the questions that matter most. But first, a quick word about how to ask them.

Don't fire them off like a checklist. Use them as conversation starters. When your doctor says, "I'm prescribing semaglutide for your weight," try: "I'd love to understand how this works for someone like me. What does the evidence say for people in my situation — my age, my health history, my other medications?"

The question is the same. But the framing turns it from a quiz into a dialogue. And dialogue is where shared understanding lives. <!-- claim: C-041 -->

Here are the questions worth asking, wrapped in the kind of language that keeps the conversation open:

**"Help me understand how this peptide works for my specific situation."** This opens the door to your doctor explaining the mechanism, the evidence, and the relevance to you — all in one breath. It's better than asking "is it FDA-approved?" as a yes-or-no, because it gets you the *why* behind the approval.

**"What would success look like, and when would we know?"** This defines the finish line. Weight loss of what percentage? Pain reduction of what degree? Over what timeframe? Without a shared definition of success, you won't know whether the treatment is working when you come back in three months.

**"What's our plan B?"** This is the question that separates thoughtful medicine from hopeful guessing. What if the peptide doesn't work? What if side effects are too much? What if insurance denies coverage? A good doctor has answers to all three.

**"Where does this come from, and how do I know it's real?"** For FDA-approved drugs, this is straightforward — they come from a pharmacy. For compounded peptides, you're asking about the pharmacy's credentials, whether it's a 503A or 503B facility, and whether they provide a Certificate of Analysis showing purity and potency testing.

**"What's the real cost — not just what I pay today?"** Beyond the copay, ask about prior authorization requirements, step therapy (where insurance requires cheaper drugs first), and what happens if coverage is denied. The sticker price doesn't matter. Your out-of-pocket cost does.

Patient-centered care isn't just about what the doctor tells you. It's about respect for your values, coordinated care, education, physical comfort, emotional support, continuity, and access. <!-- claim: C-041 --> These questions cover every one of those dimensions.

## How to Have the Hard Conversations

There's a conversation that comes up more often than any other, especially with the peptides we covered in Chapter 3. You bring up BPC-157 or MOTS-c, and you get one of three responses:

"That's not FDA-approved."

"I've never heard of it."

"Why are you reading internet nonsense?"

I want to prepare you for each one, because they're not dead ends. They're forks in the road.

**If your doctor says "that's not FDA-approved":** Your response is: "I understand. I'm not asking you to prescribe something illegal. I'm asking whether there's a legitimate pathway — a compounding pharmacy, a clinical trial, a referral — to access it under supervision." The key is to validate their caution while keeping the door open. Most doctors aren't opposed to innovation. They're opposed to risk they can't evaluate. Give them a framework they *can* evaluate.

If they still hesitate, try: "I'm not looking for off-label prescribing that makes you uncomfortable. I'm asking whether there's a specialist you'd refer me to, a compounding pharmacy you trust, or a way to work within guidelines I don't know about."

**If your doctor says "I've never heard of it":** This is actually the most honest response. Peptide science moves fast. A doctor who graduated medical school ten years ago may never have learned about MOTS-c or SS-31. Your response: "Would you be open to reviewing the research with me? I can share what I've found." Bring printouts from PubMed, not Reddit. A doctor who sees real evidence — peer-reviewed studies with PMIDs attached — is far more likely to engage than one handed a printout of a forum post.

**If your doctor dismisses you:** This is the hardest one. Some doctors are gatekeepers by personality. Some are burned out. Some have seen too many patients fooled by internet scams, and they've lost patience. Your response: "I hear your concern — I don't want to be misled either. I've tried to be careful about my sources. Here's what I've found, and I'd value your professional opinion." If they still dismiss you without engagement, you may need to find a different doctor. That's not failure. That's knowing when a partnership isn't possible.

### What If Your Doctor Says No?

Sometimes you'll do everything right and your doctor still says no. Clarify: "Is this because you believe the treatment is unsafe, or because you don't have enough information to guide me?" If it's about evidence, ask what study would change their mind. If it's outside their comfort zone, ask for a referral or find a new provider. A second opinion isn't an insult — if the "no" is based on unfamiliarity, it's the right call.

### Building a Partnership

The goal of these questions is to build a relationship, not win an argument. Start small: thank your doctor for engaging, follow up with a brief summary email, and be honest about everything — other peptides, side effects, doubts. Before each visit, note what's improved, what hasn't, and what questions have come up. Share the wins and own the setbacks. A patient who says "help me figure it out" is a partner, not a problem.

A "no" with a good explanation isn't permanent. Say: "If the evidence changes, can we revisit this?" Most doctors will say yes. Come back in six months. Not every doctor is equipped for peptide therapy — finding one who is may take effort, but it's worth it.

## When Insurance Says No: The Appeal

Let's talk about the moment nobody prepares you for.

Your doctor writes the prescription. You take it to the pharmacy. The pharmacist runs your insurance. And the system comes back with a single word: *denied.*

Insurance denial for peptide therapy is common. Insurers don't like paying for expensive new drugs when cheaper alternatives exist. But denial isn't the end of the road — it's the start of a process.

**The language of appeal:** The most effective appeals use three elements. First, medical necessity — why this specific drug is needed for this specific patient. Second, failure of alternatives — which cheaper treatments have already been tried and failed. Third, supporting documentation — lab results, diagnostic codes, and peer-reviewed literature that support the prescription.

Here's what that looks like in practice. Your doctor's office sends a letter that includes:

- The specific diagnosis and diagnostic code
- A statement that the prescribed peptide is medically necessary for that condition
- Documentation of prior treatments that didn't work (tried and failed)
- Citations from peer-reviewed literature supporting the use of this peptide for this condition
- A letter from you describing how the condition affects your daily life — because insurers respond to patient stories, not just clinical data

You can help your doctor write this letter by providing your own narrative: "Before this treatment, I couldn't walk up a flight of stairs without stopping. I've tried diet, exercise, and two other medications. None worked. Here's how this condition affects my work, my family, and my health." That story, attached to the clinical documentation, is surprisingly powerful.

If the first appeal is denied, most insurers offer a second-level appeal — often reviewed by an independent physician who wasn't involved in the original decision. That second review has a higher success rate. Don't give up at the first no.

## Where to Go for Help

You don't have to navigate this alone. There are organizations and resources dedicated to helping patients access the therapies they need.

**Compounding pharmacy verification:** The FDA maintains a list of registered 503B outsourcing facilities. State pharmacy boards track 503A pharmacies. If your doctor says "this is from a compounding pharmacy," ask which one and verify it's licensed and in good standing.

**Patient advocacy organizations:** For specific conditions — diabetes, obesity, chronic pain, osteoporosis — there are patient advocacy groups that provide education, financial assistance, and sometimes even legal support for insurance appeals. The Obesity Action Coalition, the American Diabetes Association, and the National Osteoporosis Foundation all have resources specifically for patients navigating treatment access.

**The FDA MedWatch program:** If you experience a serious side effect from any drug — peptide or otherwise — report it to the FDA through the MedWatch program. This isn't just about you. It's about building the evidence base that helps other patients. Every report adds a data point to the system.

**Clinical trial registries:** ClinicalTrials.gov lists every registered clinical trial in the United States, including trials for peptides not yet approved. If you want access to a peptide that isn't available through your doctor, a clinical trial may be your best option — and you'll be contributing to the science that helps everyone else.

## The Asking Is Harder Than the Knowing

When we started this book, Maria didn't know what a peptide was. She'd been handed a confusing prescription and a flimsy pamphlet, and she closed her laptop more confused than when she opened it.

Seven chapters later, she's different. She knows what GLP-1 receptors do. She understands the difference between a Phase 2 trial and FDA approval. She can explain why BPC-157 is promising but unproven, why 503A matters for compounded drugs, and why the PCAC's July 2026 vote on regulation could affect her access for years.

But knowing isn't the same as acting. For Maria, the hardest part wasn't reading this book. It was bringing it into the exam room.

She did, though. She brought her questions. She asked about success metrics and alternatives. She asked the compounding questions. She asked about the backup plan. And her doctor — to her surprise — was glad she asked. Not because every answer was easy, but because the conversation was better. They made a plan together. A real one. With timelines and fallbacks and shared understanding.

Maria got her prescription. She also got something more important: a partnership with her doctor. And that partnership — built on the questions in this chapter — is what will carry her through the months ahead, through the monitoring appointments and the dose adjustments and the inevitable moments when things don't go exactly as planned.

Three months later, Maria walked into her follow-up and the nurse didn't have to ask how she was doing. She'd lost the weight. Her blood work was better — A1c down, inflammation down, energy up. But what struck me most was what she said: "For the first time, I felt like my doctor and I were on the same team. She asked *me* questions. She wanted to know what I was experiencing, not just what the scale said."

That's the transformation this chapter is really about. Not getting a prescription. Not winning an argument. Building a relationship where your voice matters as much as the data on the clipboard.

The best patient is an informed patient. You've done the hard work. You've read this book. You understand the science, the evidence, the risks, and the regulations.

Now take that knowledge into your appointment. Ask the questions. Have the hard conversations. Appeal the denial. Find the advocate. Your doctor works for you, and you deserve answers.

Maria's journey started with confusion and ended with a partnership. Yours can too.
